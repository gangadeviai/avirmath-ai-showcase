# Linear vs Logistic Regression in Machine Learning: Which Model, When, on Real Data (Credit Risk and Demand Forecasting)

**When should you use linear regression and when logistic regression in machine learning?** Use linear regression when the target is a number (demand, price, duration) and logistic regression when the target is yes or no (default, disease, churn); use softmax for several classes. Both fit a weighted sum of features, logistic through a sigmoid, and both give a readable coefficient per feature, which is why they underwrite credit scoring and clinical risk scores. Fit them first as the baseline, judge them on held-out data with the right metric (RMSE or MAE for a number, AUC and calibration for a probability), and watch for the four classic failures: extrapolation, data leakage, class imbalance with an unchosen threshold, and a non-linear relationship visible in the residuals. This lesson does all of that on 1,000 real German loans and 17,379 real hours of bike rentals.

## Key facts
- **When should I use linear regression and when logistic regression?** Linear regression when the target is a number you want to predict (rentals per hour, price, length of stay). Logistic regression when the target is a yes or no and you want a probability (loan default, disease, churn). Softmax when there are several classes.
- **What must the dataset look like?** One row per example, one column per feature that was known at decision time, and one target column. Hold out part of the rows (here 200 of 1,000 loans, 20 percent of the bike hours) to test the model on data it never saw.
- **How do I know the model worked?** For a number: mean absolute error, root mean square error and the variance explained on the held-out rows. For a probability: accuracy against the trivial baseline, area under the ROC curve, and calibration (when it says 30 percent, do about 30 percent go bad?).
- **What are the main ways regression fails in practice?** Extrapolating outside the training range, leaked features that already contain the answer, class imbalance with an unexamined threshold, and a non-linear relationship that shows up as a pattern in the residuals.
- **How do I read a logistic regression coefficient?** e to the coefficient is an odds ratio: how the odds of the positive class multiply per unit increase of that feature, holding the others fixed. On the German Credit data a better current-account balance roughly halves the odds of a bad loan.
- **Is a neuron the same as logistic regression?** Yes. A weighted sum of inputs plus a bias, passed through a sigmoid and trained by gradient descent on cross-entropy, is one neuron, and the last layer of a classifier is a logistic or softmax regression on learned features.

Let me start where a working data scientist starts, with a decision somebody has to make. A bank receives a loan application and has to decide whether to lend. Behind that decision sits a table of past loans, and the one we will use is real: 1,000 loans from a German bank, published for research, with twenty facts about each borrower and a final column that records whether the loan was repaid or went bad. Seven hundred were repaid, and three hundred were not. Before any formula, notice what this table must contain, because it is what you will need for your own problem. Each row is one example, each column is a feature you knew at decision time, and one column is the target you want to predict. We will keep eight features, such as the balance of the current account, the loan duration, the amount, the savings and the age. And we will hide 200 rows from the model, so that at the end we can test it on loans it has never seen. Which model, then? The first question is what kind of answer you need. If the target is a number, such as how many bikes will be rented at five o'clock, that is a regression problem, and linear regression is the place to start. If the target is a yes or no, such as will this loan go bad, that is classification, and logistic regression is the place to start. If there are several classes, you reach for softmax, which we covered last time. The second question is what you need from the answer. When a regulator or a doctor has to understand the decision, a linear or logistic model gives you a readable coefficient for every feature, and that readability is often the reason it wins. When the relationship is clearly not a straight line and you have the data to prove it, a tree ensemble or a neural network will fit better. But fit the regression first, because it is the baseline every other model has to beat, and because the last layer of that network is a logistic regression anyway. So let us build linear regression properly, on five points small enough to do by hand: x from one to five, and y equal to two, two point eight, four point one, four point nine, and six point two. Any line, w x plus b, misses each point by some residual, and if we square those residuals and add them up we get a single number, the loss, that says how badly the line is doing. Drag the line, and the loss changes with it. To find the best line we set both derivatives of the loss to zero, which gives two equations in two unknowns, and they solve in closed form. The slope is the covariance of x and y divided by the variance of x, and the intercept is chosen so that the line passes through the mean point. With our numbers, x bar is 3 and y bar is 4. The covariance sum comes to 10.5 and the variance sum to 10. So w is 1.05, and b is 4 minus 1.05 times 3, which is 0.85. The loss at that line is 0.075, down from 91.1 for a flat line through zero. For more than one input the same idea is written once, in matrix form: X transpose X, times theta, equals X transpose y. These are the normal equations, and solving them gives the plane that fits a cloud of points in three dimensions, or the hyperplane that fits a table with six columns, which is exactly what we are about to do. And gradient descent, from lesson one, arrives at the same answer. The gradient of the loss is twice the residuals weighted by x, and twice the residuals on their own. Four hundred small steps from zero, and w is 1.05, b is 0.849: the closed form, found by walking downhill. Now the real job. A bike-sharing company wants to know how many bikes will be rented in a given hour, so it can move bikes to where they are needed. The data is real: 17,379 hours over two years, and for each hour the temperature, the humidity, the wind, whether it was a working day, and the number of rentals, which averaged 189. The hour of the day goes in as a sine and a cosine, so that eleven at night sits next to midnight rather than twenty-three steps away. We solve the normal equations on 13,903 training hours and read the coefficients. Each extra degree of temperature adds about 6.7 rentals an hour, and each point of humidity removes about one. Those are sentences the operations team can act on, and no deep model hands them to you so directly. Did it work? On the 3,476 held-out hours the typical error is 97 rentals, the root mean square error is 140, and the model explains 0.42 of the variance. That is a useful baseline and an honest one, and we will come back to what it gets wrong. Now the yes-or-no question. Here are ten students, the hours each one studied, and whether they passed. A straight line predicts numbers below zero and above one, which no probability can be. So we take the linear score and pass it through the sigmoid, one over one plus e to the minus score, and now the output lives between zero and one. Read it backwards and the score has a meaning. The log of p over one minus p, the log-odds, is exactly w x plus b, so each extra unit of x multiplies the odds by e to the w. That is why doctors and banks like this model: every coefficient is an odds ratio they can read aloud. How do we fit it? Not with squared error. We maximise the likelihood of the labels we actually saw, the product of p for every pass and one minus p for every fail. Take the log, flip the sign, and the loss is the cross-entropy from last lesson, for two classes. Differentiate, and the sigmoid's derivative cancels the log's. The gradient is X transpose times p minus y: prediction minus truth, weighted by the inputs, exactly the softmax rule. There is no closed form this time, so gradient descent does the work. Fitted, w is 2.6 and b is -7.16. The fifty-percent point is 2.75 hours; two hours of study gives a 0.12 chance of passing, and four hours gives 0.96. Back to the bank. We standardise the eight features so that each is measured in standard deviations, then train logistic regression on the 800 loans with nothing but the gradient we just derived. The loss per loan falls from 0.693 to 0.483. Now the readout a credit officer actually uses. Holding everything else fixed, a better current-account balance multiplies the odds of a bad loan by 0.47, so it roughly halves them. A longer duration multiplies them by 1.35, a larger amount by 1.27, and more savings by 0.71. Every one of these is a sentence you can defend in front of a regulator. Did it work? On the 200 unseen loans the model is right 74 percent of the time, against 71 percent for simply approving everyone, and its area under the curve is 0.74, which is where published results on this dataset sit. The calibration error is 0.074, so when it says thirty percent, about thirty percent go bad. And here is one real applicant from the test set: 35 years old, a 36-month loan of 6,948 marks, with a current account under 200 marks. The model puts the risk at 56 percent, and the bank's threshold decides what happens next. Now the part most explanations skip: when these models fail. First, extrapolation. The hottest hour in the bike data was 41 degrees. Ask the model about sixty degrees and it happily predicts 577 rentals, more than at thirty, because a straight line never stops. A regression is only trustworthy inside the range it was trained on. Second, leakage. The bike table also records casual and registered rentals separately, and the two add up to the total. Put them in as features and the error drops to exactly zero, which looks like a triumph and is actually a model that needs the answer in order to make its prediction. Whenever a result looks too good, ask whether a feature was known before the decision was made. Third, imbalance and the threshold. Seventy percent of the loans are good, so approving everyone is already 71 percent accurate, and accuracy alone would hide that. At a threshold of one half the model catches 48 percent of the bad loans; lower it to zero point three and it catches 65 percent, at the cost of flagging 87 applicants instead of 49. The threshold is a business decision about which mistake costs more, not a mathematical constant. Fourth, the wrong shape. Look at the bike model's errors by hour of day. At eight in the morning it under-predicts by 237 rentals, at five in the afternoon by 160, and at three at night it over-predicts by 34: it cannot bend around rush hour. When the residuals show a pattern like this, the relationship is not linear, and that, not fashion, is the signal to move to trees or a neural network. So here is the checklist. Decide what kind of answer you need: a number means linear regression, a yes or no means logistic, several classes means softmax. Build a table with one row per example, features known at decision time, and a held-out test set. Fit the regression first, read the coefficients as plain sentences, and judge it on the test set with the right metric: error for a number, area under the curve and calibration for a probability. Then check the four ways it fails: never extrapolate outside the data, hunt for leaked features when a result looks too good, choose the threshold by the cost of each mistake, and look at the residuals for a pattern before you reach for a deeper model. Do that, and you have done what the bank and the bike company actually do. Next: the neuron and activation functions.
