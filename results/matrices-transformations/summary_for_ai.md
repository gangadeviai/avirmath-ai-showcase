# Matrices in AI: The Linear Algebra Inside Every Neural Network Layer, Attention Head and Recommender

**How are matrices used in AI?** Every layer of a neural network is a matrix times the input vector plus a bias; attention computes all query-key dot products at once as Q times K transpose; an image is a matrix of pixel values and a convolution is a matrix product in disguise. A matrix is best understood as a linear transformation that moves the whole space, with its columns recording where the basis vectors land; matrix multiplication composes transformations, the determinant measures how area scales, and eigenvectors are the directions a matrix only stretches, which is what limits the learning rate. Google's PageRank is an eigenvector, Netflix-style recommenders factorise the user-by-film matrix, and LoRA fine-tunes large language models with low-rank matrices.

## Key facts
- **How are matrices used in neural networks?** Each layer computes W times x plus b: a weight matrix times the input vector. GPT-2's feed-forward layers are 768-by-3072 matrices, about 2.4 million learned numbers each, and the model's 124 million parameters are a few hundred such matrices.
- **How does attention use matrices?** Stack the queries as rows of Q and the keys as rows of K; Q times K transpose gives every query-key dot product in one product, a token-by-token grid of scores. Each of the 12 heads in a GPT-2 layer does this in parallel on the GPU.
- **What does a matrix do to a vector?** It mixes the vector's coordinates with the matrix's columns: the result is the first coordinate times the first column plus the second coordinate times the second column. Geometrically, the whole space moves and the vector moves with it.
- **Why is matrix multiplication done the way it is?** Because multiplying two matrices is the same as applying one transformation and then the other. The row-times-column rule is exactly what composition requires, and that is also why the order matters.
- **What are eigenvectors and why do they matter for training?** Directions the matrix does not turn, only stretches; the stretch factor is the eigenvalue. For a loss surface they are the principal curvatures, and the largest eigenvalue sets the maximum safe learning rate.
- **What is LoRA?** Low-rank adaptation: instead of updating a full 768-by-768 weight matrix (589,824 numbers), learn two thin matrices of rank 8 (12,288 numbers), 48 times fewer. Any matrix can be approximated by a sum of a few rank-one layers, which is the same idea behind image compression and recommender systems.
- **Where else are matrices used in industry?** Google's PageRank is the principal eigenvector of the web's link matrix; Netflix-style recommenders factorise the user-by-film ratings matrix into two thin ones; every CT slice is a 512-by-512 matrix that a convolutional network reads as matrix products.
